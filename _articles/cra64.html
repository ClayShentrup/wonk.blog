a near-axiom

welfare economics leans on an idea so intuitive it rarely gets stated: giving someone an additional option, one they're free to decline, can't make them worse off. if the new option is better for them, they take it. if it isn't, they ignore it and they're exactly where they were.

philosophers and game theorists have long known this idea has exceptions. the interesting question is where those exceptions live. one good way to find out is to test the axiom against a case where most of us already have a strong, settled intuition, and see what happens.

the case i want to use is the civil rights act of 1964. so let me be clear about what this piece is and isn't. the act was a landmark, and an enormous improvement over the legal order it replaced, under which black americans across much of the country could be refused a meal, a room, a job, or a seat, often by laws that required the refusal. nothing here proposes changing it. in this exercise, the act is the fixed point. the axiom is what's being tested.

the thought experiment

start with an idealized version of the protection: the right to be served, housed, and hired without regard to race. to keep the question clean, imagine it's enforced by some perfect, imaginary mechanism. no lawsuits, no burden of proof, no cost to the person who holds it. it simply works.

now the question. suppose that in addition to this right, each person were offered the option to receive a sum of cash instead. nobody would be required to take it. the default stays exactly as it was: everyone holds the right unless they choose otherwise.

would that world be worse?

the near-axiom says no. nothing has been taken away. for any single person, considered in isolation, an option they can decline looks like a pure gain.

why the answer is probably yes

the axiom quietly assumes that each person's choice affects only that person. here, that assumption fails in at least three ways.

an option that can be exercised can also be demanded. thomas schelling's work on strategy showed that in situations involving other people, having more options can leave you worse off, because others can pressure you to use them. if the right could be traded away, anyone inclined to discriminate could simply favor the people who had traded theirs. holding on to the right would become a disadvantage, and the "free choice" would stop being free.

the right protects more than its holder. a refusal at a lunch counter or a hotel desk is a public act. people who see it learn something about who belongs, and that lesson shapes what they come to see as normal. gordon allport's research on prejudice found the mirror image: contact between groups, under the right conditions, reduces prejudice. visible exclusion pushes the other way. a rule that holds for everyone keeps exclusion out of public life. a rule that some people have sold off lets it creep back in, and everyone pays for that, including people who never sold anything.

some rights are valuable because nobody can waive them. this is why we don't let people sell their votes or contract themselves into servitude, even when an individual seller might think the deal is a good one. the protection does its work as a shared floor. once it becomes a personal asset that can be bargained away, it stops being a floor.

so the thought experiment ends somewhere reassuring. the intuition that civil rights shouldn't be for sale isn't in tension with careful welfare reasoning. careful welfare reasoning supports it, once you account for how one person's choice changes what everyone else faces. and none of this reasoning gives any weight to the preferences of people who want to discriminate. the whole argument runs on the welfare of the people discrimination harms.

substitutes versus complements

there's one more thing the thought experiment shows. if cash can't stand in for the right, it can still sit beside it.

discrimination does two kinds of damage. one is the public, normative damage described above, which a universal legal rule is well suited to prevent. the other is economic: over a lifetime, people who face discrimination can end up with worse jobs, worse housing, and higher costs of searching for both. some of that happens in places a law can't easily reach. a hotel turning someone away is visible. a hiring decision that quietly never happens usually isn't.

a broad floor of economic support, such as a universal basic income or a refundable tax credit, can reach that remaining harm without trading anything away. it doesn't require anyone to prove intent, and it helps everyone who ends up with less, whatever the cause. the right keeps exclusion out of public life. the floor catches what gets past.

policy debates often get framed as a choice between instruments, as though supporting one means opposing the other. this is a case where that framing is false. the law and the floor do different jobs.
